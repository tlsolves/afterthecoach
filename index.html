Coaching ends.
Does the work?

Every instructional support provider shares the same goal: leaders whose instructional capacity keeps growing after direct support ends. Teaching Lab designs job-embedded support so the school owns the practice, and plans for that handoff from the first day of the partnership. Here is what we learned in District 75, La Joya ISD, and DC Public Schools (DCPS), starting in New York City, and the tools any provider or leader can take and adapt.

Funded by the Center for Public Research and Leadership (CPRL) as part of the NYC Solves PL Network, and built on Teaching Lab's Gates-funded Change Agency Guide. Everything here is public and free to use.
For instructional support providers and district and school leaders
What works
Leaders who learn alongside their coaches and carry their district's own tool into classrooms, with a full year of evidence in District 75 and measurable leader growth in La Joya ISD.
What sustains
Coherence between PL and coaching, designed deliberately before the cycle starts.
What districts own
The goals, the data, and the routines, handed off on a planned date so leaders carry the work forward with confidence.
What travels
Five design principles, tested in New York City, Texas, and Washington, D.C.
The question behind the project
What does a school own by June, and what do we design in September to get there?
The handoff is the goal of the partnership, not the end of it. That makes it something we plan for on day one rather than hope for in the spring.
What we found across every site

Where the practice transferred, the principal could name the instructional priority in two sentences.

System leaders sustain instructional improvement while leading everything else a district or school requires: staffing, budgets, compliance, and the daily pull of the urgent. Their time and attention are the most valuable resources in the work, and they deserve structures that honor them.

Across District 75, La Joya ISD, and DCPS, the common thread was focus: a shorter list, held longer. Three conditions made that focus possible, and each one is something a leader can set on purpose, early in the year.

One priority, said the same way
Leaders, coaches, and teachers reached for identical language for what they were working on.
Time the leader protected
Planning and team time kept its place on the calendar when other priorities arrived.
A signal of progress they trusted
Leaders had a checkpoint early enough in the year to act on what it told them.

Problem 01

Sustaining leader engagement
beyond the first session

Superintendent and principal energy is highest at launch. When engagement is structured into the partnership from day one, leaders build the habits that outlast direct support. The lever is design.

Home › Browse by problem › Problem 01
Understand the model
The mechanism: structured participation, anchored in the district's own tools
Teaching Lab's model designs leader engagement into the partnership structure from the start. Every leader session starts from that school's own coaching goal. PL and coaching are anchored to the tools and standards leaders already use. Coaches then reinforce the same look-for in classrooms and debriefs, so what a leader commits to in PL is what the coach carries forward. In La Joya, that meant real-time, side-by-side coaching inside leaders' own PLCs.
Why coherence is the lever
Leaders lean in when PL, coaching, and their evaluation responsibilities feel like one body of work. When all three share the same language, the same tools, and the same priorities, leaders experience participation as part of their role rather than an addition to it.

About La Joya ISD
Located in the Rio Grande Valley (RGV), La Joya ISD is a bilingual school district with a strong emphasis on Dual Language programming for K to 5 students. District enrollment: 23,995 students. Teaching Lab provided school leadership coaching across 21 elementary schools.
What Teaching Lab did
Codified leadership learning community
Structured coaching grounded in shared metrics and curriculum-aligned tools
Real-time, side-by-side coaching inside PLCs, rather than briefings before and after, built capacity faster. The focus was data analysis tied to HQIM priorities, using indicator 5.4 of the Texas principal standards (T-PESS) and the PLC Vertical Rubric as shared metrics across all 21 schools.
Leadership engagement with instructional support
Leaders observed and debriefed alongside Teaching Lab coaches
Joint observation cycles using the district's Spot Check tool and the Texas teacher standards (T-TESS) gave leaders a structured lens for classroom visits. By mid-year, leaders named 2 of 3 effective facilitation actions with no coach present.
The artifact that made it work
The Alignment Crosswalk mapped the whole system in one place
Principal standards (T-PESS), teacher practice (T-TESS and Spot Check), and student outcomes (Demonstrations of Learning, unit assessments, and the state assessment blueprint), all in one coherent artifact. Leaders could see how their participation connected to classroom results, making the model legible rather than just logical.
What it taught us
Using the tools leaders are already evaluated on made coaching feel like part of the job, not an extra initiative.

Guiding question
How can school leader coaching be structured across a district system to strengthen leadership practice, instructional coherence, and student outcomes?
1
Diagnose
What instructional, leadership, and system conditions must be understood to effectively align coaching support across the district?
We began by developing a deep understanding of the district context through a district-level needs assessment, leadership interviews, and artifact reviews to identify instructional priorities, leadership strengths and needs, and next areas of growth. School leaders also participated in joint learning walks, which served as both a diagnostic and relationship-building structure. Leaders observed instruction together, engaged in facilitated debrief conversations, and identified trends in instructional practice and areas for refinement across campuses.
2
Identify Metrics and Anchor the Work
What leadership, instructional, and student outcome metrics will anchor coaching, professional learning, and implementation monitoring?
Using findings from the needs assessment and learning walks, we collaborated with district leaders to identify the metrics that would anchor leadership coaching, professional learning, and progress monitoring across the system.
Leadership Practice
Used the state principal evaluation rubric (T-PESS) to support leader self-reflection and identify leadership indicators most aligned to the project focus.
Teacher Practice
Used the district observation tool, Spot Check, aligned to the district's instructional lesson structure and T-TESS indicators to monitor instructional implementation and teacher practice.
Student Performance
Used district-created standards-aligned formative assessments, including Demonstrations of Learning (DoLs), Short Constructed Responses (SCRs), and observational evidence of student thinking, writing, reading, and discourse.
Aligning Coaching and Professional Learning
Professional learning and coaching were intentionally designed as one connected system to ensure leaders and teachers experienced consistent instructional priorities, language, and expectations across all support structures.

1

2

1

Build collective understanding of the instructional skill, concept, or priority through modeled examples of effective leadership and instructional practice.
2

3

Engage leaders in role-play, rehearsal, and feedback scenarios to practice coaching conversations and instructional feedback.
Progress Monitoring
Each professional learning session included aligned progress monitoring structures connected to leadership practice, teacher practice, or student outcome metrics to reinforce accountability, measure implementation, and guide next steps.
Individualized Campus Coaching Support
Coaches provided differentiated campus support aligned to school-specific needs, including redelivery of professional learning content, co-planning PLCs, joint classroom observations, instructional modeling, rehearsal support, and leadership coaching connected directly to implementation data.

Case study · District 75 · New York City

District 75: the integrated model,
running here

Leader professional learning aligned to the instructional focus, reinforced through Teaching Lab coaching, and carried into classrooms. This is the scope of work model, whole-group professional learning integrated with job-embedded coaching, running in an NYC Solves district with a full year of data.

What makes D75 a distinct context
District 75 is an NYC Solves district and NYC Public Schools' citywide special education district
D75 serves students with disabilities across all five boroughs. Unlike a geographically bound district, D75 is made up of schools and programs located across many different buildings and communities. Teaching Lab's work spanned 13 schools across 21 sites, several of them high school and transition programs. Students are supported through highly specialized instructional settings designed to meet a wide range of learning, communication, behavioral, and physical needs. Some students participate in traditional state assessments with accommodations, while others participate in alternate assessments.
In the context of NYC Solves, this requires attention to both access and instructional integrity. The goal is meaningful access to grade-level mathematical ideas, in ways that are responsive to each student's needs, while keeping the mathematics intact.
The terms school and site operate differently here than in most districts
In D75, a school is tied to one DBN and has one principal, a team of assistant principals, and instructional coaches, and often includes multiple sites. A site is the smaller building-based community where students, teachers, APs, and coaches experience the work day to day. In many ways, a D75 site functions like a school in other districts, while a D75 school functions more like a small, micro-district.
This structure makes the connection between leader professional learning and coaching impact more complex. Leader learning has to travel across multiple layers before it shows up consistently in site-level implementation, which is exactly what makes D75 an important case for understanding what that travel requires.

The theory of action in District 75
Leaders learned in professional learning that started from their own school's coaching goal. They placed current practice on the NYC Solves Progression of Practice and named a look-for. Their Teaching Lab coach then reinforced that same look-for in classrooms and debriefs, using the same tool. By June, teachers reported leaders who knew the curriculum and protected the time, and classrooms showed students doing more of the math.
What Teaching Lab did
01 · Leader PL, then a named commitment
Leaders started from their own goal and placed it on the Progression
Four virtual sessions for school leaders, aligned to the instructional focus, each starting from that school's own coaching goal. Leaders chose a lesson component, located current practice on the NYC Solves Progression of Practice, and named one concrete look-for, using the same tool coaches use.
02 · Reinforced through coaching
Coaches carried the same look-for into classrooms
Each school's Teaching Lab coach reinforced the leader's look-for in classrooms and debriefs, using the same Progression of Practice, across 12 coaching visits per site. What a leader named in PL became the thread the coach followed in the building.
The tool that connected PL to coaching
The NYC Solves Progression of Practice appeared in both spaces
The same tool leaders used in their professional learning sessions was the tool coaches brought into classrooms and debriefs. That shared anchor is what made coherence possible: leaders deepened their use of one framework instead of translating between two, placing instruction on a developmental continuum and naming concrete next moves.
How the model works: step by step
This is the sequence Teaching Lab designed across four leader PL sessions in District 75. Each step builds on the last, moving leaders from a school's coaching goal to a named look-for their coach reinforces in real classrooms.
1
Leader PL begins with the coaching goal
The leader PL is designed to connect school goals, coaching priorities, and classroom practice.
A strong coaching goal names:
Teacher-Facing Goal
What teachers are working to improve.
Student-Facing Goal
What students should be doing differently.
Classroom Look-For
What success looks and sounds like during instruction.
This model starts with the coaching goal because the goal anchors the entire cycle. A focused goal is one leaders can observe for, coaches can support consistently, and everyone can see changing classroom practice.
2
From coaching goal to classroom evidence
Leaders use PL time to understand the goal deeply enough to recognize it in classrooms.
During PL, leaders:
1
Review their school's coaching goal
2
Name evidence of where they have seen the goal in classrooms
3
Identify the lesson component most connected to the goal
4
Connect that component to the Progression of Practice
The goal is for leaders to connect their school's actual coaching focus to evidence they can look for during instruction. In D75, where leader learning has to travel across schools, sites, APs, and site-specific coaches, this model creates a concrete bridge from leader PL to coaching practice.
3
Naming where practice is, and what comes next
The Progression of Practice gives leaders and coaches shared language for describing current practice and identifying the next step.
Leaders use the Progression of Practice to:
1
Select a Lesson Component
Where does this goal show up in the lesson?
2
Self-Assess Current Practice
Where are we on the continuum?
3
Name a Look-For
To move from ___ to ___, I will look for ___.
This step matters because it makes the coaching goal actionable. Leaders move from a general aim to a specific one: naming the part of the lesson where the goal should show up, placing current practice on a continuum, and identifying what they will look for next.
4
Reinforced through coaching, carried into classrooms
The look-for a leader names in PL is the look-for the coach reinforces in the building.
What the coach plans from
  • the leader's selected lesson component
  • the leader's self-assessment on the Progression of Practice
  • the leader's named look-for
In classrooms and debriefs
The Teaching Lab coach reinforces the same look-for, using the Progression of Practice, across 12 coaching visits per site, and debriefs in the same words the leader used in PL.
Leader PL and coaching work as one system. PL prepares leaders to take part in coaching with shared goals, shared evidence, and shared tools, and coaching carries that learning into classrooms. This is where the model connects to the theory of action: leader PL becomes job-embedded when coaching reinforces it in a structured observation cycle.

Case study · DCPS · Washington, D.C.

Aligning coaching and professional learning
across an urban district

DC Public Schools (DCPS) is a Teaching Lab district partnership outside NYC Solves, which extends the evidence beyond one city. When common coaching trends inform a shared PLC learning arc, instructional leaders build coherence together, aligned to district frameworks.

Home › Case studies › DCPS
The PL and coaching approach
The core design
At DCPS, coaching and professional learning were intentionally designed as one connected support system. Common trends identified across coaching partnerships informed a PLC learning arc that allowed instructional leaders to collaboratively analyze shared problems of practice, strengthen instructional leadership, and build coherence aligned to the DCPS LEAP Leader Framework (LLF) indicators.
The process
1
Coaching Team Analysis and Data Review
Coaches analyzed mid-year leadership and implementation data to identify the LLF indicators with the greatest opportunity for growth. Coaching teams reviewed agendas, observation trends, artifacts, and quantitative data to surface common leadership priorities and instructional trends across schools.
2
Develop the Learning Arc
Based on identified trends, a four-session PLC arc was designed that chunked learning into manageable, high-leverage instructional leadership practices. Each session focused on actionable strategies leaders could immediately apply within coaching conversations, PLCs, and classroom observations.
3
Coaching as Reinforcement
PLC sessions created structured opportunities for instructional leaders to pause, reflect, collaborate, and strengthen their practice. Coaching cycles then reinforced the professional learning by providing leaders with individualized support, feedback, rehearsal, and implementation guidance connected directly to the session focus.
Components of each PLC session (60 minutes)
Connect to the problem of practice
Sessions began by grounding leaders in a shared instructional or leadership challenge connected to implementation data, coaching trends, or student outcomes.
Problem Analysis
Instructional leaders analyzed coaching artifacts, classroom scenarios, curriculum materials, and observation evidence to identify instructional priorities, determine root causes, and plan leadership responses aligned to the featured LLF indicator.
Application and Action Planning
Leaders used planning tools and structured protocols to determine how they would implement the learning within upcoming walkthroughs, PLCs, coaching conversations, or teacher support structures.
Components of effective professional learning for instructional leaders (90 to 120 minutes)
1
Model the Instructional Component
Facilitators modeled the featured instructional strategy or curriculum component to establish a clear exemplar of practice. Leaders experienced the strategy as learners while identifying the teacher moves and conditions that supported successful implementation.
2
Identify Look-Fors and Align to Teacher Practice Metrics

The model · Organizational leadership

The conditions that make
coherence possible

Teaching Lab's integrated model is a set of deliberate design choices for how professional learning and coaching connect so that leaders build lasting capacity. These principles have traveled across New York City, Texas, and Washington, D.C. They can travel further.

Our theory of action
The question: How do we build sustainable district-level math learning that keeps leaders at all levels engaged in high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) implementation and in reinforcing job-embedded support?
If school and district leaders actively and consistently participate in curriculum-aligned professional learning communities and structured observation cycles, using NYC Solves tools and protocols, then they can independently reinforce instructional priorities and begin to build a math learning community that sustains coaching impact beyond direct job-embedded support.
The shared language is already yours: the NYC Solves Progression of Practice, the observation and feedback protocols, and All Minds on Math. The operative word is independently, and the design principles below are how Teaching Lab plans for that independence from day one.
How Teaching Lab approaches this work
The Change Agency Guide, and where this project sits inside it
Teaching Lab's Gates-funded framework for implementing HQIM names four shifts a system makes. This project is the next phase of Shift 2.
Shift 1
Investment and understanding
Everyone can say why this curriculum, in their own words.
Shift 2 · This project
School leadership team development
Leaders build the instructional vision, systems, and coaching skill the work runs on.
Shift 3
Teacher preparation and support
Standards study, unit unpacking, lesson internalization, coaching.
Shift 4
Data-informed practices
Cycles that show progress early enough to act on it.
Each shift has three levels of progression, so a system can name where it is today rather than start from zero. The Coherence Workbook's starting point levels follow the same three-level idea.
Five design principles
The part that travels. These are what we would ask any leader to build, whatever the curriculum or the district.
01
Coherence
One priority, traced from vision, to look-fors, to professional learning, to coaching, to evidence.
PL and coaching are aligned by explicit intention before a cycle begins. The same priority, language, and look-fors appear in whole-group sessions, in coaching debriefs, and in the observation tool leaders already use. Where the practice transferred, the principal could name the priority in two sentences. In the Coherence Workbook, Steps 1 to 3 are that trace, and coherence statement 1 checks it.
What sustains
02
Participation
Leaders are named participants in the learning, using the same tools as their coaches.
Leaders are learners in this model, as well as sponsors of it. In La Joya, principals were coached live inside their own PLCs. In District 75, leader sessions started from each school's own coaching goal, and coaches reinforced the leader's look-for with the same NYC Solves Progression of Practice. In the workbook, leader PL sits beside teacher PL in Step 3, and Leadership Practice is its own goal type.
What works
03
Sustainability
Every routine is built to be handed off, with a date for the handoff.
The handoff is the goal of the partnership, not the end of it. Teaching Lab names which routines leaders will own by year's end, scaffolds toward that transfer throughout the coaching cycle, and builds the data habits that make independent monitoring possible. The question at the start of every partnership is: what will leaders own, and lead, when we step back? The Handoff column in Step 2 of the workbook holds the answer.
What sustainsWhat districts own
04
Ownership
The district holds the goals, the data, and the meeting. We hold the mirror.
The tools, data, and facilitation routines Teaching Lab builds are designed for district ownership. District roles sit in the Owner column of the workbook, and the district team runs the Step 4 reflection, with partners as thought partners. Teaching Lab's role shifts from doing, to coaching leaders to do, and eventually to coaching them to coach others.
What districts own
05
Entry point
The work is startable from wherever a school is today, at any point in the year.
Every school is growing from somewhere. The workbook's three starting point levels let targets fit each school, so every school's growth stays visible, not just the strongest school's. You can start now: name one priority in one sentence, decide what you will look at in six weeks, then work backward to the goal. A short first cycle beats a long first plan.
What travels

The replicables are these design principles. Any instructional support provider implementing HQIM can ask: Have we designed coherence between our PL and coaching? Are leaders participants in our model as well as sponsors of it? Have we named what the district will own, and by when?

Make a copy
Are our priority, professional learning, and coaching pointing at the same thing, and is it actually happening?
  • 1Priority and look-fors. Write one priority sentence, choose 2 to 3 look-fors from the district's own observation tool, and name where each school is starting.
  • 2Goals and KSIs. Set year-end goals, then the key success indicators (KSIs) you expect each quarter for teacher growth, student learning, and leadership practice, each with an evidence source, an owner, and a handoff.
  • 3Checkpoints: PL + coaching. For each KSI, name what you will see and when, plus the PL session and coaching move that build it, side by side.
  • 4Reflect. Rate six coherence statements and answer four questions as a team. The statement with the most room to grow becomes next quarter's focus.

Change Planbook

Make a copy
When a leader needs more time in classrooms, where does that time come from?
  • 1Define the change. What is changing, what stays the same, and what you will watch for.
  • 2Know your people. Map who the change touches, their stance, and their influence.

The six coherence statements · Step 4 of the workbook
Each statement describes one link in the line from priority to classroom. Rate each one for a school as In place, Partly, or Not yet, then ask which one you would build first in the next six weeks.
1
Same words everywhere
The priority and look-fors appear word for word in the PL materials, the coaching notes, and the observation tool.
2
Same skill, same month
Teachers and leaders learn the same skill in the same month. Teachers learn to do it. Leaders learn to spot it and coach it.
3
Leaders practice, not just listen
Leader PL includes rehearsing a coaching conversation or a feedback moment.
4
Coaching follows the session
Between sessions, coaching builds on the skill that was just taught.
5
Every KSI has evidence and an owner
Teacher growth, student learning, and leadership practice each have a source, a checkpoint, and a person responsible.
6
Targets fit each school
Goals build from where each school is starting, so every school's growth is visible.
